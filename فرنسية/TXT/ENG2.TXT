

## The Frog Prince

By Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm

In the olden time, when wishing still helped, there lived a king whose daughters were all beautiful, but the youngest was so lovely that even the sun, who had seen many strange things, marveled every time he shone upon her face.

Near the king's castle there was a great dark forest, and in the forest, under an old linden tree, there was a well. When the day was very hot, the king's daughter would go into the forest and sit by the cool well. To pass the time, she would take a golden ball, throw it up in the air, and catch it. This was her favorite toy.

One day, the golden ball did not fall back into her little hands. It fell on the ground, rolled, and landed in the water. The princess looked down into the well, but it was so deep that she could not see the bottom. She began to cry bitterly.

As she wept, a voice croaked: "What is the matter, princess? Your tears would melt even a stone."

She looked around and saw a frog sticking his ugly head out of the water.

"Oh, it's you, old water-splasher," she said. "I am crying because my golden ball has fallen into the well."

"Be quiet and stop crying," said the frog. "I can help you. But what will you give me if I bring your toy back?"

"Whatever you want, dear frog," she said. "My dresses, my pearls, my jewels, even my golden crown."

The frog replied: "I don't want your clothes, pearls, or jewels. But if you will love me and let me be your companion, sit beside you at your little table, eat from your golden plate, drink from your cup, and sleep in your little bed, then I will dive down and bring your golden ball back."

"Oh yes," she said. "I promise you everything you ask, if only you bring me my ball." But in her heart she thought: "This silly frog is talking nonsense. He sits in the water with the other frogs and croaks. He can never be a companion to a human."

The frog dove down. After a while, he swam back up with the golden ball in his mouth and threw it on the grass. The princess was overjoyed. She picked it up and ran away as fast as she could.

"Wait, wait," cried the frog. "Take me with you! I can't run as fast as you." But the princess did not listen. She ran home and soon forgot about the poor frog.

The next day, while the princess was eating dinner with the king, she heard a strange noise at the door: plop, plop, plop. Then a voice called: "Princess, youngest princess, open the door for me!"

She ran to the door and saw the frog. She was terrified and slammed the door shut. Back at the table, the king asked her what was wrong.

"There is a horrible frog outside," she said. "He brought my golden ball back from the well, and I promised him he could be my companion. But I never thought he could leave the water. Now he is here, and he wants to come in."

The king said: "What you promise, you must keep. Go and let him in."

She opened the door, and the frog hopped in. He followed her to her chair and said: "Lift me up beside you." She hesitated, but the king made her do it.

When the frog sat on the table, he said: "Push your golden plate closer so we can eat together." She did, but she was disgusted.

After he had eaten, he said: "Now carry me to your little room and make your bed ready so we can sleep."

The princess began to cry. She was afraid of the cold frog. The king grew angry and said: "Do not despise someone who helped you in need."

So she picked up the frog with two fingers and carried him upstairs. She put him in a corner of her room. But when she lay down, the frog hopped to her bed and said: "I am tired. Lift me up, or I will tell your father."

This was too much for the princess. In anger, she grabbed him and threw him against the wall with all her might.

"Now you will be quiet, you ugly frog!" she said.

But when the frog fell to the ground, he was no longer a frog. He was a handsome prince with kind eyes. He told her that a wicked witch had cast a spell on him, and only the princess could break it by throwing him against the wall. The prince became her companion, and they lived happily ever after.
